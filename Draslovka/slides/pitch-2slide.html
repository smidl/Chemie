Steps a physics check could accept
Of eleven proposed reaction steps, ten could not even be submitted to a quantum-chemistry calculation as written.

The tools are not ignorant of your chemistry. They are being fed malformed inputs.

Every failure we traced had a bookkeeping cause, not a chemical one — a scoring test asking the wrong question, one arrow standing for several reaction steps, or a missing byproduct or counter-ion. Two completely independent methods, a language model and a quantum-chemistry calculation, flagged the same three causes. That agreement is why we are confident in the diagnosis.

What we would build, in what order

A feasibility layer that is honest about what it can and cannot judge

Judging whether a reaction will actually work is a question of rates, not just energies — and rates are expensive. We have measured exactly how expensive, and what you can and cannot economise on.

The load-bearing finding: reaction energies are cheap and essentially solved. Reaction barriers cannot be shortcut — we tried, and the shortcut is off by a factor of five. So the only way to afford them is triage: run the expensive rung only where the cheap rungs disagree. Knowing which shortcuts fail is itself the result.

Two phases

Phase 1 · starts now · no data needed

Make your routes checkable, then score them

  • The missing plumbing. Turn planner output into complete, balanced, single-step reactions — the thing that took our physics check from 1 usable step out of 11 to 8. Unglamorous, and nothing downstream works without it.
  • A reliability map. For N molecules you choose: scored routes, a stated operating point, and an explicit "we cannot judge this" bucket. A gate that hides its blind spots is worse than no gate.
Phase 2 · unlocked by data

Your chemistry, where no public tool is competent

  • Coverage where public data is thin — cyanide and nitrile platform chemistry, quaternary salts, charged and organometallic species.
  • Real conditions. Feasibility depends on solvent, catalyst and temperature. Public data mostly does not record them; a process plant necessarily does.
  • The no-precedent frontier. For chemistry with no literature analogue, a physics calculation is the only available judge — this is where the ladder above becomes the product.

What we would need from you — and it is not your IP

The gap is not clever methods. It is the ordinary bookkeeping that public reaction databases systematically discard — which is precisely why ten of eleven steps could not be checked.

  • Complete equationsFull stoichiometry, byproducts and counter-ions included.
  • The conditions you actually ranSolvent, catalyst, temperature, concentration.
  • The failures tooWhat did not work is worth more than what did — nobody publishes it.

You already hold all three, because you run these reactions at plant scale. A public database records that a reaction happened; your records say what actually went in, under what conditions, and how often it failed. That difference is the whole gap.
